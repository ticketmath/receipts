# #008 · The Hormuz bypass: how much of the lost oil did the pipelines make up for?

**Video:** coming soon

## The answer

From late February to mid-September 2026, close to **2.8 billion barrels** of oil exports that would have sailed out of the Strait of Hormuz never did. The extra oil sent around the strait by pipeline made up for more than **500 million barrels** of that: **17.9%**. For every barrel the bypass made up for, about 5.6 were lost. Both figures come from the same International Energy Agency (IEA) commentary.

The pipes did run hard. Saudi Aramco says it took its East-West pipeline to full capacity, 7 million barrels a day, and that the switch took 8 days. They still covered only a small part of what the strait used to carry.

Container ships had no pipe at all. From July to September 2026, on 64 days of the quarter (69.6%), ship tracking recorded no container ship in the strait.

## How we counted

- **The main number** is the IEA's own pair of figures (commentary of September 18, 2026, with provisional Kpler tanker-tracking data to September 12): the oil the *higher* flows through the bypass ports, Yanbu on the Red Sea and Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman, made up for since the war began, divided by the oil exports the strait lost over the same period. The IEA rounds both ("more than 500 mb", "approaching 2.8 billion barrels"), so the exact share can move a little.
- **"The bypass"** means the two pipelines around the strait: Aramco's East-West line across Saudi Arabia to Yanbu, and the UAE's line to Fujairah. Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain have none.
- **Before the war**, we use the EIA (June 2025) and the IEA factsheet (February 2026) for spare pipeline room, and the EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook for quarterly flows through the strait. EIA and IEA define flows a little differently, so no ratio here divides one by the other.
- **Container ships** are daily counts from IMF PortWatch (AIS signals), averaged by quarter.
- **Prices** are EIA series: daily Brent around the start of the war, and monthly averages for February and September 2026.

## Before the war: how much room the pipes had

| | Million barrels a day |
|---|---|
| Oil through the strait, Q4 2025 (EIA) | 21.6 |
| East-West pipeline capacity (EIA) | 5 |
| East-West, temporarily expanded in 2019 (EIA) | 7 |
| UAE pipeline to Fujairah (EIA) | 1.8 |
| Both pipelines together | 6.8 (31.5% of the strait's Q4 2025 flow, if they had been empty, which they weren't) |
| Spare room available in a disruption, EIA estimate | 2.6 (12.0% of the strait's flow) |
| Spare room, IEA estimate | 3.5 to 5.5 |
| East-West already in use in early 2026 (IEA) | 2 |
| UAE pipeline already in use (IEA) | 1.1, with 0.7 of extra room |

The pipes were not sitting empty: they already carried oil every day. And not every barrel fits: in March 2026 Saudi Arabia shut in about 2.5 million barrels a day, mostly offshore medium and heavy crude that cannot go through the East-West line (IEA, April 2026). Kuwait, which sent about 2.5 million barrels a day through the strait in 2025, "has no options to bypass Hormuz" (IEA, March 2026).

## The bypass in 2026 (IEA)

| | Million barrels a day |
|---|---|
| Exports via Yanbu and Fujairah, February 2026 | 4.1 |
| Peak, June 2026 | **7.8** (up 3.7, about 1.9 times February) |
| August 2026, after Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping | 5.5 |
| Average offset since the war began | 2.8 a day |
| Oil through the strait before the war (IEA) | 20.7 |
| Oil through the strait, August 2026 | 7.6 (36.7% of pre-war) |
| Oil through the strait, September 2026 (IEA, October report) | 10.9 (52.7% of pre-war) |

All Gulf oil exports, by every route: 24.5 million barrels a day in February, 8.7 in March (35.5% of February, pipelines included), and 16.1 in June (67.1% of the pre-war average of 24).

The EIA's quarterly table tells the same story: 21.6 million barrels a day through the strait in Q4 2025, 14.9 in Q1 2026 (69.0%) and 4.9 in Q2 2026 (22.7%), about 16.7 million barrels a day lost.

## At the pump

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Brent, daily (last trading day before the war; peak since) | $71.32 | $138; $115 on the latest day published |
| Brent, monthly average, February and September 2026 | $70.89 | $114 (up $43.27 a barrel) |
| U.S. regular gasoline, monthly average | $2.91 | $4.36 (up **$1.45 a gallon**) |
| U.S. Gulf Coast jet fuel, monthly average | $2.26 | $4.39 (up $2.13 a gallon) |

In 2024, only **7% of U.S. crude imports** (0.5 million barrels a day) came through Hormuz (EIA). Prices rose anyway because oil is traded on a world market, which the EIA describes as a kind of global auction: when supply disappears anywhere, buyers everywhere bid for what's left. The EIA does not attribute the whole rise to Hormuz, and neither do we: the video shows the timing, not a measured cause.

## Gas had it worse

About 20% of the world's liquefied natural gas (LNG) trade went through the strait in 2024, mostly from Qatar, and 83% of it went to Asia (EIA). 93% of Qatar's LNG exports pass through the strait (IEA). LNG through the strait went from 10.5 billion cubic feet a day in Q4 2025 to 0.8 in Q2 2026: **7.6%** of what it was.

## The box

**Ships through the strait (IMF PortWatch, daily AIS counts, quarterly averages)**

| | Container ships a day | All ships a day |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 2025 | 12.8 | 70.8 |
| Q2 2026 | 0.8 (6.2% of Q4 2025) | 8 |
| Q3 2026 | 0.5 (4.0% of Q4 2025) | 6.2 |

By the same count, tankers in Q2 2026 were 8.3% of Q4 2025, far below the EIA's oil flow: AIS signals undercount traffic in a crisis, which is why the oil numbers come from the EIA and the IEA, not from ship counts.

**The last time container ships lost a sea route**, in 2024, they sailed around Africa: Bab el-Mandeb went from 18.5 container ships a day in 2023 to 5.4 in 2024, and the Cape of Good Hope from 6.2 to **19**. There is no ocean route around Hormuz: a box for Dubai has to be unloaded at a port outside the strait and finish the trip by road (Dubai Customs set up a sealed-truck corridor from Oman).

**What crossing costs**

| | |
|---|---|
| War-risk insurance before the war (The National) | 0.25% of the ship's value |
| War-risk insurance in July 2026 (Marsh, via The National) | 3% to 10% (12 to 40 times) |
| Maersk emergency increase to the Gulf, 20-foot container | $1,800 |
| Maersk emergency increase, 40-foot container | $3,000 |
| Maersk emergency increase, reefer, special or dangerous cargo | $3,800 |
| Maersk fee per container if the ship crosses Hormuz | $1,000 |
| **A 40-foot container whose ship crosses the strait** | **$4,000 extra** |
| Maersk cost recovery surcharge to the UAE (contracts only) | $500 |

**Jebel Ali** (Dubai), from DP World's results: the group's throughput (42,826 thousand TEU in the first half of 2026, 45,438 a year earlier) minus its throughput without Jebel Ali (39,681 and 37,664). That leaves **3.1 million** twenty-foot boxes in the first half of 2026 against 7.8 million in the first half of 2025: 40.5%. The half still includes January and February, two normal months, and DP World's count includes the boxes trucked in from Oman (3.1 million).

In October, Maersk relaunched its FM1 service to the region, calling at Khor Fakkan, on the Emirates' other coast, outside the strait: 30 days from Qingdao on the first sailing.

## What this number doesn't show

- The IEA's figures use provisional tanker-tracking data to mid-September 2026, and August was still provisional. The IEA revises earlier months in every report.
- Since late February 2026 the EIA has warned that AIS signals in Hormuz are unreliable: oil flows may be revised, and PortWatch's ship counts undercount traffic.
- Before the war, estimates of spare pipeline room disagreed: about 2.6 million barrels a day (EIA) against 3.5 to 5.5 (IEA). The actual rise at the June peak fell inside the IEA's range. The IEA noted that sustained flows at Aramco's 7 had not been tested.
- The September flow comes from the IEA's October report and the pre-war base from its September commentary, so the September share is approximate. By September oil was moving through the strait again (with ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman), and the East-West line, struck in early September, was running again later that month. The main number covers only late February to mid-September.
- The EIA's quarterly table ends in Q2 2026; Q3 is not out yet.
- Fuel prices rose after the strait closed, but neither the EIA nor this video puts a number on how much of the rise came from Hormuz.
- The surcharges are Maersk's. Other lines charge different amounts, and what each box pays depends on the route and the contract.

## Reproduce it

```
python calc.py
```

Standard library only, no network. It reads the files in `data/`:

- `published_figures.csv`: every figure taken from a publication (EIA, IEA, Saudi Aramco, Maersk, DP World, The National), with its unit, a description and the source URL;
- `portwatch_daily.csv`: IMF PortWatch daily ship counts used: the Strait of Hormuz from October 2025 to September 2026 (container ships, tankers, all ships) and Bab el-Mandeb and the Cape of Good Hope in 2023 and 2024 (container ships); an empty cell counts as zero;
- `eia_prices.csv`: the EIA price series used (daily Brent since February 2026; monthly Brent, U.S. regular gasoline and U.S. Gulf Coast jet fuel for 2026).

It prints the consistency checks (EIA and IEA flows agree within 10% where they overlap, the June rise in bypass flows sits inside the IEA's pre-war estimate of spare room, every quarter of ship data is complete, DP World's totals are consistent) and writes `numbers.json`.
